I often wonder how people who speak high-resource languages have the best communication technologies by their side. The other day, I wanted to send holiday wishes to my friends and family in my native language, Kannada, but my keyboard kept converting my words into the wrong Kannada script, and Google Translate wasn’t any better. So, I ended up typing the same message in Kannada, but in English letters. The minute I transliterated my words, the emotion and excitement in my voice were instantly flattened. It felt like every other message that I send to anyone on any normal day. 

Kannada is a morphologically complex language. A whole sentence in English can be merged into a single word in Kannada. It is a Dravidian language, a sibling of Tamil, with centuries of borrowing from Sanskrit. For languages like English, it is easy to break the words into suffixes and only understand the root word. This makes it easier to train models too and it’s also what makes low-resource languages which do not follow linguistic structures like English difficult for a model to learn and understand. When technology is built mainly for speakers of a few languages, it makes others unable to express their emotions and perspectives the right way.

The downstream effect of burying the voices of people is far more detrimental than just the inability to use technology. When people switch to English to express their opinions, there is less data online about these languages, leading to poor software tools and people being discouraged from using the language itself. Speaking in English also makes us lose the nuances, humor and phrases commonly used in the language. Progress has been very slow, and for everyday users like me, the tools still fall short.

Kannada is a language that has survived for centuries, only to feel invisible after a few years of English-first tech. Despite these challenges, I believe that Kannada will endure, not because technology will save it, but because the speakers keep choosing it.
